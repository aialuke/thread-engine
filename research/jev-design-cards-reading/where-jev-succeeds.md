## 2. What the successes have in common

Reading down the table, every result with hard numbers shares most of these:

- **High repeat volume of the same question shape.** Every quantified win reruns one fixed question (or small fixed battery) across tens to thousands of items: 1,200 re-ranking calls, 488 skill-suggestion requests, 450 entity pairs, 990–1,312 judge-benchmark items, 300×3 triage tickets, 2,000 wine reviews. The two cookbooks with no real numbers (guardrails, RAG classification) are also the two run on a handful of hand-picked illustrative examples rather than a real corpus.
- **Small, closed answer space per call.** Noul (yes/no), a few-level Score, or Choice among a handful to a few hundred fixed, named options — never open-ended judgment. Even the 218-way and 182-way and 75-way Choice cases are one clean selection among *named, enumerated* options, not free reasoning.
- **Compact, single-purpose state.** A pair (`query, candidate`), a claim + one section, an entity pair, one ticket, one filing's business section — not a long multi-part brief with many live variables at once.
- **A checkable answer exists somewhere** — a benchmark label, a removed citation's true target, an expected leaf node, a human-annotated intent, a critic score, a regex-bounded span. Every case that reports accuracy (not just "here's a confidence number") has this. The cases with no fixed labels (semantic search, guardrails, RAG passages, autoformat, function calling) report *confidence numbers* and face-validity, never accuracy.
- **Thresholds fit empirically on held-out or labelled data**, not guessed once: the CMU paper's τ is fit on a selection set and re-validated on an extension; skill suggestion's 0.30 gates are fit on the labelled 488; classification-by-confidence's 0.9 cutoff is read directly off the accuracy-vs-confidence curve for that dataset. Cookbooks that use round numbers picked once (0.7/0.35, 0.8, 0.2/0.5) are the demo-scale, non-benchmarked ones.
- **Confidence is used to route, not to decide the hard cases.** The recurring pattern — cookbooks and the CMU paper alike — is "accept when confident, escalate/flag/ask when not": date extraction, classification-by-confidence, guardrails, the entity-alignment curator queue, the CMU cascade (τ=0.9 keeps 99.6% of GPT-6's accuracy at 47% of its fee). Confidence never functions as a standalone verdict on a genuinely uncertain, one-shot call.
- **Independent numbers agree with vendor numbers on this shape**: the CMU paper (peer-reviewed, blinded human adjudication) and the independent triage blog post both reproduce the pattern — cheap and accurate on repeated, closed, checkable decisions; the CMU paper is explicit that the advantage *disappears* once checking requires a multi-step derivation or resisting a well-written wrong answer (JudgeBench: reasoning 68.4% vs GPT-6's 95.9%; coding 76.2% vs 97.6%).

## 3. Where it struggles — documented limits and reported failures

**Vendor's own jaggedness page** (`docs.typesafe.ai/model-jaggedness/jev-1.13`, quoted exactly):
- Literal reading: "answers the question you wrote, not the one you meant. Scoping words, negations, and implied conditions are read at face value."
- Math/numbers: "Jev is not a calculator. We strongly recommend implementing any mathematical logic in code."
- Counting: "does not count reliably... characters in a word, occurrences of a term in a passage, and items in a long list."
- Numeric closeness: "cannot reliably judge whether two values are near each other."
- Date ordering: "reads dates as text, not as ordered quantities... unreliable."
- Indirection: "Instructions carrying double negatives or complex indirection are answered less reliably."
- Large irrelevant state: "Accuracy falls as the state grows with content unrelated to the decision. Unrelated detail acts as a distractor."
- Adversarial content: "does not treat it as hostile by default... can move the answer."
- Contradictory instructions/criteria: "jev-1.13 might get confused."
- Generation: "is not trained to generate text... will not work well and will be very slow."
- Coding-agents page is explicit it is "**not** a drop-in replacement for the LLM behind Claude Code, Cursor, opencode, Copilot" — no streaming, no code generation, no conversation ([docs.typesafe.ai/introduction/coding-agents](https://docs.typesafe.ai/introduction/coding-agents)).

**Independent (CMU paper)**, beyond the accuracy gaps already in the table:
- Gap widens sharply exactly where a derivation must be checked or a well-written wrong answer resisted: JudgeBench reasoning 68.4% vs GPT-6's 95.9% (n=98), coding 76.2% vs 97.6% (n=42); RM-Bench hard style-adversarial pairs -19.8pp (74.8% vs 94.6%).
- Reference-free natural prose judgment: 52.5% (near chance for every judge tested, not just Jev), error-detection AUROC 0.518 — "no threshold helps." Jev stays confident (mean max-probability 0.90) while wrong.
- Confidence doesn't transfer across workloads: a temperature fit to sharpen RewardBench (0.65) softens JudgeBench and HaluEval (2.15, 4.45) — "no single temperature fits; each workload needs its own validation."
- Order/framing sensitivity: reversing candidate order flips 3.25% of RewardBench and 11.14% of JudgeBench decisions.
- One frozen cascade policy (GPT-5.6 fallback, τ=0.7) lost 2.35pp, "beyond its two-point tolerance" — the accept/escalate rule is not guaranteed to transfer even within the paper's own tested policies.

**This repo's own prior work** (`research/jev-design-cards-reading/`) already found framing, order, and abstain-option bias in one-off Choice votes over long state — not re-run here, cited as already-established.

**Community/HN**, weaker evidence:
- `hbrn` on the Doom demo: "Jev cannot play tic-tac-toe or checkers, but can play Doom... You can build a harness where a coin flip is playing Doom" — argues the harness, not Jev, does the cognitive work, and faults TypeSafe for sharing "no harness, no control loop, no state encoding, no prompts" ([HN 49816019](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49816019)). This is skepticism about vendor framing, not a measured failure, but it's the one dissenting independent voice found.
- No HN/Reddit thread surfaced a documented failure with numbers on a one-off, high-option, long-state judgment — the gap this repo's design-card votes fall into. Searched specifically for this; found none (see §5).

## 4. Fit check — the repo's three uses

**X post filtering (Experiment 3).** Bulk, repeated, closed yes/no questions over compact per-post state, with agreement against a model panel standing in for a checkable answer (58.1% decided at 92.6% agreement). This matches essentially every feature behind the table's successes: high volume, small answer space, compact state, an external check to validate against. The result (concrete yes/no stable, graded questions drifting under rewording) is exactly what the jaggedness page and the CMU paper predict — literal, closed decisions hold up; anything closer to a graded judgment degrades. Good fit, and the internal result is consistent with the wider evidence rather than an outlier.

**Design-card votes (one-off UI decisions).** The opposite profile on nearly every axis: a single call, not a repeated batch; 4–5 open-ended options rather than a small closed binary; long, multi-part state (a whole design brief) rather than a compact pair; and no checkable answer to validate a threshold against — the 0.50 confidence-floor rule was fixed once, not fit on labelled local data the way every successful cookbook's threshold was. This is the precise shape the CMU paper warns about ("validate the cascade on the kind of pairs it will meet," "no single temperature fits") and the shape this repo's own design-cards-reading study already found biased by framing and order. Poor fit — the operator's bad experience matches what the evidence would predict, not a fluke.

**Shadow-mode referee hook.** Structurally closer to the successful shape: closed Choice/Noul questions (`route`, `disposition`, `in_scope`/`extra_deletion`/`needs_human`), compact capped state (diff lines, file lists, token ceilings in `jev/thresholds.yaml`), and — critically — it runs in shadow mode, logging a would-be decision against what actually happens, before any threshold is trusted to act. That is exactly the CMU paper's own checklist: validate locally before extending the envelope, and treat confidence as an escalation signal to check, not a certificate. Its current thresholds (0.50/0.70/0.85 in `jev/thresholds.yaml`) are engineering defaults, not yet fit against the shadow log the way the cookbook thresholds were fit against labelled data — so the fit is good in structure, contingent on that shadow data actually getting reviewed before `mode: active`.
