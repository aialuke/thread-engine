# Reader: human survey methodology and decision science

Lens: what decades of human research say about how the question writer steers the answer, translated into claims testable on Jev.
Read-only. Nothing in the repo was edited. Scratch downloads are in `scratchpad/hm/`.

Source notes: "opened" means I read the full text, or the abstract where the table says so. Where I could only reach a secondary source, the Strength column says **secondary**. Effect sizes are copied from the source.

Question key: Q1 framing (evidence, risk note, lean) · Q2 order and names · Q3 abstain design · Q4 repeat stability · Q5 structured descriptions.

| F4 | A real midpoint is different from "don't know". Offering a midpoint improved reliability. | F5 | Satisficing: under cognitive load, respondents pick the first reasonable option, agree with what the question asserts, endorse the status quo, or say "don't know". | Krosnick 1991, *Applied Cognitive Psychology* 5:213–236. | F6 | Acquiescence: people endorse whatever the question asserts. Agree/disagree items are less valid than forced choice between competing views. The effect averages about 10%. | Same chapter as F2 | "On average, 14% more people agreed with an assertion than expressed the same view in the corresponding forced choice question … taken together, these methods suggest an acquiescence effect averaging about 10%." "answers to these sorts of questions are less valid and less reliable than answers to the 'same' questions expressed in a format that offers competing points of view" | Q1 | A stated lean works like an assertion to agree with. Adding "current lean: X" raises P(X), and asking "Should we do X?" (yes/no) gives a higher P(X) than a forced choice with X listed among rivals. | Remove the lean. | F7 | Response order: with visual (read) presentation, early options win (primacy); with oral presentation, late ones win (recency). [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1086/269029). Abstract read via OpenAlex. | "In visual presentation, primacy effects have been found … In studies involving oral presentation, nearly all response order effects have been recency effects." "counterbalancing order creates a new problem: variance in responses due to systematic measurement error" · (1987 abstract) "we test some predictions … (a primacy effect) and among which respondents it should be strongest (those low in cognitive sophistication). Which direction (primacy or recency) is an empirical question for a model reading text, and the human visual/oral split does not decide it. The effect grows with option count and card length. | Run every rotation (or a Latin square) and report the averaged probability plus the position spread. Don't trust a single order. | Replicated across many split-ballot studies |

| F10 | Framing: equivalent outcomes described as gains or as losses flip the choice (72/28 against 22/78). | F11 | Status-quo framing: the option shown as the current state gets extra share. | F13 | Choice under conflict: when two options are close and attractive, more people defer or take the default. | F15 | Decoy effect: adding an option that one existing option clearly beats raises the chance of choosing the option that beats it. | Huber, Payne & Puto 1982, *J. Consumer Research* 9:90. | F16 | Analysis of Competing Hypotheses (ACH): judge every item of evidence against every hypothesis. Drop evidence that doesn't tell the options apart. Try to disprove, not prove. ## 2. Contradicts our plan

1. **More abstain options may be worse, not better (F1, F2, F23).** The main human finding is that don't-know options invite satisficing and don't improve reliability. Splitting "ask the human" into two gives two cues. Krosnick & Presser recommend forcing a substantive answer and then measuring *strength* separately. On the model, the equivalent is: pick among the real options, and derive "too close" from the probability margin. That favours the plan's alternative (a separate "does any option fit?" check) over a split in the menu. It also suggests the separate check should be a strength or confidence read, not another multiple-choice item. **Caveat:** Krosnick & Presser also warn that strongly worded filters intimidate (F3), so a separate "does any option fit?" call must be worded neutrally.
2. **But "too close" has human support as a genuine midpoint (F4, F13, F14).** Midpoints improved reliability, and conflict between close options really does raise deferral. The literature supports "too close" as a legitimate category and treats "none fit" as the satisficing risk. It supports an asymmetric design more than a symmetric split: keep "too close" (validate it against the margin, F14) and move "none fit" into the separate check.
3. **A neutral risk note is not enough (F18, F9).** "Be fair and unbiased" type instructions corrected less than consider-the-opposite, and a lean labelled random still anchored experts. The plan's "neutral risk note" should become a "fails if …" line for *every* option, written in the same frame (F10).
4. **Pruning dead options needs a rule (F15 against F16).** Pruning is supported, because dominated options act as decoys. But ACH warns against dropping options that are *unproven* rather than *disproved*. If the card writer prunes by judgement, that brings back the writer steering the plan is trying to remove.
5. **Counterbalancing order doesn't remove order bias; it spreads it (F7).** Averaging over rotations is right. The plan should also report the position spread as its own number, not only the average.
6. **High model confidence doesn't show there was no framing (F9).** Experts' certainty didn't vary with the anchor. Don't use confidence as the steering detector. Only paired manipulation arms can show steering.

## 3. Searched, found nothing (or not opened)

- **Q1:** Schweiger, Sandberg & Ragan 1986 (AMJ) and Schwenk 1990 (the devil's advocacy / dialectical inquiry meta-analysis): the publisher pages returned 403, and I found no open copy or abstract I could read, so neither is cited. I found no human study that manipulates a written *recommendation inside the question stem* in a multiple-choice ballot. The anchoring and acquiescence findings (F6, F8, F9) are the closest.
- **Q2:** I found no human study on *option names* (short labels versus descriptive titles) as distinct from order. Tourangeau, Rips & Rasinski (2000) is a book; it wasn't opened and isn't cited.
- **Q3:** I found no human study that splits "no opinion" into "none fit" and "too close" in one item. The closest are opinion floating (F24) and Tversky & Shafir's conflict against dominance (F13).
- **Q4:** I found no human test–retest study with exact repeats and no memory, which is the model's situation. The human three-wave designs (F23) are an analogy only.
- **Q5:** Bettman & Kakkar 1977 (the information-format matrix against the by-brand list) is paywalled and not opened. Slovic & MacPhillamy 1974 is not opened (I used the Kivetz & Simonson restatement). Delphi primary sources (Dalkey 1969 RAND RM-5888) returned 403, so F22 rests on a textbook.

## 4. Hand-offs

- **LLM choice bias reader:** Tjuatja et al. found that RLHF models are *less* sensitive to human-style wording biases but *more* sensitive to non-bias perturbations. They report "RLHF-ed models tend to show a larger magnitude of effect sizes among the non-bias perturbations" ([arXiv 2311.04076](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04076), §3.2). This predicts that Jev may resist classic acquiescence while still being moved by formatting noise, so the experiment needs perturbation-only control arms.
- **LLM abstention and calibration reader:** F2's "report the answer, then its strength" is the human version of selective prediction with a threshold. F9 (confidence stays the same while the pick moves) is a human analogue of miscalibration under a prompt anchor. It is worth checking whether the LLM literature shows the same thing.
- **TypeSafe docs reader:** check whether Jev exposes per-option probabilities (needed for F2, F11b and F14's margin analyses) and whether option descriptions can be structured fields rather than strings (F17, F23).

## 5. Card-format checklist (from the strongest findings)

1. No stated lean, recommendation, "current" or default label on any option (F6, F8, F9, F11, F12).
2. The question is a choice among competing options, never "confirm X?" (F6).
3. The same fields for every option, all filled in; write "none known" instead of leaving a field out (F17, F23).
4. Fixed decision criteria appear before the options (F17, F20).
5. The evidence is an option × item matrix (for, against, or neutral per option). Drop items that don't separate the options (F16).
6. Every option gets a "fails if …" line in the same frame; no single overall risk note (F10, F18, F19).
7. No evaluative adverbs or adjectives from the writer ("clearly", "safer", "obviously") (F22).
